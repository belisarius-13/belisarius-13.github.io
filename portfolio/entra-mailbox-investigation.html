MFA succeeded. Who changed the mailbox?

Joseph Daniels · 17 November 2026 · Identity investigations

At 14:03, Microsoft Entra ID recorded a successful sign-in with MFA satisfied. Nine minutes later, the same user’s mailbox had a new rule that moved payment-related messages to RSS Feeds and marked them as read. If I were triaging the first event in isolation, I might ask whether the user was travelling. The second event gives me a much better question: who wanted those messages out of sight?

This field note follows a fictional exercise from David Rushing’s BHIS ActiveSOC webcast. The times and events come from the exercise. Here’s how I would reason through them at the analyst’s desk.

Start with the nine-minute gap

The first sign-in came from an unfamiliar network. Two minutes later, a non-interactive sign-in for the same account appeared from a different address with a client the exercise describes as new for this user. A network change has ordinary explanations, including a VPN. I would compare the application, device and recent sign-in history before deciding how much weight to give it.

At 14:09, the directory audit log records an authenticator registration. This changes my next step. I would contact the user through a trusted channel and ask a specific question: did you register an authenticator at that time? I would also check which session performed the registration. A user can recognise their morning sign-in and still have no knowledge of a later change.

Read the rule itself

In Exchange audit data I would inspect the New-InboxRule event, including the conditions, destination folder and read status. An ordinary filing rule is plausible. A rule that singles out invoices and payments, moves them to a rarely opened folder and marks them as read could keep the owner from seeing a supplier’s reply. That gives the SOC a business reason to prioritise the investigation.

To connect Exchange activity to Entra, I would look for the session ID in both records. Where available, the audit record’s AppAccessContext.AADSessionId can be compared with the sign-in session ID. Without a shared identifier, I would record the account and time match as circumstantial. Even a matching session tells me which session acted. Identifying the person using it takes more evidence.

Follow the impact beyond the mailbox

At 14:20, the exercise shows a SharePoint file shared with external guests. A minute later, the corresponding notification was moved out of Sent Items. I would preserve both records and identify the recipients, permissions and any recorded file access. An invitation tells me who could be affected. It does not establish that anyone opened the file.

The exercise closes at 14:43 with session revocation, a password reset, removal of the added authenticator and inbox rule, and notification of recipients. My handover would also identify who will review the sharing link and check subsequent sign-ins. The rule, authentication method and shared file are separate items to account for when verifying containment.

What I’d carry into a Sentinel investigation

I would write down three things before closing the alert: the strongest link between the sign-in and mailbox change, the business impact I can substantiate, and the evidence I still need. Here, the rule explains why payment correspondence might disappear. The audit trail alone cannot tell me whether a payment was diverted.
